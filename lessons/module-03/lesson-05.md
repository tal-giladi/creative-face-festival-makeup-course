# 03.5 · Color combinations that always work

Beginner+ · 60 minutes. Some designs look great and some look messy, even with the same brush work.
Very often the difference is the colors. In this lesson you learn four color combinations that
always work, a simple rule for how much of each color to use, and how to keep a palette readable on
any skin tone. Then you paint one small design in three palettes: the module's final practical.

## How it works

A **palette** is the small set of colors one design uses. Three or four colors are plenty. Four kinds
of palette always work, and you can find each one on the color wheel from
[lesson 03.1](lesson-01.md):

- **Analogous:** three neighbors on the wheel, like red, orange and yellow, or blue, purple and pink.
  Calm and easy, and they blend cleanly in a gradient.
- **Complementary:** two opposites, like blue and orange, purple and yellow, red and green. Strong and
  lively. Use one as the main color and the other in small touches. Don't sponge them into each
  other: the middle turns brown ([lesson 03.2](lesson-02.md)).
- **Monochrome + accent:** one color in light, middle and dark (tint, pure, shade), plus one small
  accent in a different color. Elegant and hard to get wrong.
- **Warm or cool:** red, orange and yellow feel **warm** (sun, fire, tiger); green, blue and purple
  feel **cool** (ice, water, night). A mostly warm design with a little cool, or the other way round,
  looks lively.

How much of each color matters as much as which colors. A simple guide is **60-30-10**: about 60% of
the painted area in the **main color**, 30% in a **second color** and 10% in an **accent**, the small
details that catch the eye. Black outlines and white highlights ([lesson 03.3](lesson-03.md)) don't
count; they go with everything.

Finally, colors look different on different skin. On **deep skin**, light and bright colors like
yellow, orange and pastels can look thin and see-through. Paint a thin layer of **white underneath
first**, let it dry, then the color: it will be bright and solid. On **light skin**, very pale colors
and white can disappear; give them a black outline or use a deeper shade. Strong colors with a black
outline read well on every skin tone.

## Step by step

1. **Pick a palette type** for the design: analogous, complementary or monochrome + accent.
2. **Find the colors on the wheel** and write them on a palette card: main, second, accent.
3. **Mix what you don't have** on the plate ([lesson 03.1](lesson-01.md)), and paint a swatch of each
   color on the card.
4. **Check the skin tone.** Painting on deep skin? Mark which light colors need white underneath. On
   light skin? Mark which pale colors need an outline.
5. **Paint the main color first** (the biggest area, about 60%), then the second color (about 30%).
6. **Add the accent last** (about 10%): small dots, a center, a sparkle.
7. **Outline and highlight** in black and white, as in [lesson 03.3](lesson-03.md).
8. **Step back and compare.** Does one color clearly lead? Does the accent catch your eye?

## Practice exercise

Make **three palette cards** (15 minutes) on the `palette-cards` sheet or three paper squares.

1. Card 1, analogous: three neighbors, e.g. blue, purple and pink.
2. Card 2, complementary: two opposites, e.g. blue and orange; mark which one is the accent.
3. Card 3, monochrome + accent: one color in light, middle and dark, plus one accent.
4. On each card, paint the swatches and fill in the 60-30-10 bar: which color gets 60, 30 and 10.

Stop when each card has its swatches, its type written on it and a filled 60-30-10 bar.

<details><summary>Hint</summary>

Not sure if two colors are opposites? Find one on the wheel and look straight across. Not sure which
should be the accent? Pick the brighter, more eye-catching one: accents work best small.

</details>

## Mini-project

**Module 3 practical: one design, three palettes.** Paint the small flower design from the
demonstration (a five-petal flower, two leaves, a swirl and three dots) three times on the
`three-palettes` sheet, once with each of your palette cards. Use what you learned in every lesson of
this module: mix the colors you need on the plate (03.1), sponge a soft base under one of them if you
like (03.2), finish all three with black outlines and white highlights from one light direction
(03.3). Then choose your favorite and paint it on your forearm, or on both cheeks as a mirrored pair
using the face map (03.4).

You're done when:

- [ ] All three versions have the same shapes; only the palette changes.
- [ ] Each version follows its palette type and roughly 60-30-10, with a small accent.
- [ ] No muddy colors: mixes are clean and opposite colors are not sponged into each other.
- [ ] Outlines are thin-thick and every highlight is on the same side.
- [ ] Your favorite is painted on skin and easy to read from arm's length; if you painted both
      cheeks, the two sides match.

## Common beginner mistakes

| What happens | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Design looks messy and loud | Too many colors, all used equally | Three or four colors; one clearly leads (about 60%) |
| Accent doesn't stand out | Accent used almost as much as the main color | Keep the accent to small touches, about 10% |
| Brown patch between two colors | Complementary colors sponged into each other | Keep opposites apart; blend only neighbors |
| Yellow, orange or pastels look thin on deep skin | Light colors are see-through over dark skin | Paint a thin layer of white underneath first and let it dry |
| Pale colors vanish on light skin | Too little contrast with the skin | Add a thin black outline or use a deeper shade |

## Optional challenge

Paint the same flower design a fourth time with a **warm and cool** palette: warm petals (orange and
red) with a single cool accent (a blue center and blue dots). Then swap: cool petals with one warm
accent. Which feels like summer and which like winter?
