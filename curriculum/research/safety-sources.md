# Safety & Regulatory Sources: Face Paint and Festival Makeup

Check date: 2026-10-07. Each URL below was fetched on this date unless marked otherwise.
"Loaded" = page fetched and title read. "Search only" = page would not load in the fetch tool; the facts come from the search-engine index of that page. "Unconfirmed" = I could not verify the fact on a primary source.

## 1. FDA (United States)

Key facts for the course:
- Every color in a cosmetic (face paint and theatrical makeup included) must be FDA-approved **for that specific use**. A color allowed on nails or hair may not be allowed on skin.
- A color may not be used **around the eyes** unless its regulation says so. Most certified dyes (FD&C / D&C numbers) are NOT approved for the eye area.
- Fluorescent ("neon") colors: only a handful are approved, and **none of them may be used near the eyes**. FDA lists them as D&C Orange No. 5, 10, 11 and D&C Red No. 21, 22, 27, 28 (the Novelty Makeup page also adds D&C Yellow No. 7, making eight).
- Glow-in-the-dark: **luminescent zinc sulfide is the only approved glow color**. It is allowed only in externally applied facial makeup and nail polish, at most 10% in facial makeup, for "limited, infrequent occasions, e.g., Halloween". Facial products must be labelled "Do not use in the area of the eye."
- Believe a "not for use near the eyes" label even when the packaging photo shows the product near the eyes.
- Patch test: put a little on the arm a day or two before (FDA says watch for redness, rash, swelling).
- Remove makeup the way the label says, gently near the eyes.
- Kohl / kajal / surma / al-kahl: illegal color additives in the US, often high in lead; FDA knows of lead poisoning in children from them.
- Henna: approved **only as a hair dye**, not for direct skin application. "Black henna" often contains PPD, which is not allowed in cosmetics applied to skin and can cause dangerous reactions.
- Decorative (costume) contact lenses sold without a prescription are illegal and can cause serious eye injury or blindness.
- Glitter: FDA's fact sheet gives "holographic" glitter (aluminum, an approved color, bonded to an etched plastic film) as an example; every component must comply with color additive rules. FDA never says craft glitter is fine for skin. A direct FDA consumer statement that "craft glitter is not for skin" was NOT found: unconfirmed.
- Spirit gum / eyelash adhesives: no current FDA consumer page specifically about spirit gum or lash glue was found (unconfirmed). Novelty Makeup page only says follow the label to remove glue from fake beards etc.
- Lead in cosmetics: FDA's concern for this course is mainly kohl (above). General lead guidance not researched further.
- Reactions: see a doctor, and report to FDA (MedWatch / cosmetic complaint).

## 2. European Union

Key facts:
- EU cosmetics law is Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. Colors are a positive list in **Annex IV**: only listed colorants may be used.
- Annex IV gives each colorant a field of use and conditions, e.g. "Not to be used in eye products", "Not to be used in products applied on mucous membranes", "Rinse-off products" only. So a color legal on cheeks may be illegal on eyelids or lips.
- Ingredients are labelled by Colour Index number (e.g. CI 77491 iron oxide). Learners can look up any CI number in **CosIng**, the Commission's ingredient database.
- Microplastics restriction, Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2055 (in force 17 Oct 2023):
  - **Loose plastic glitter for arts, crafts, toys etc. banned from sale since 17 October 2023.** Stock already on the market that day did not have to be withdrawn.
  - Loose plastic glitter sold **as a cosmetic product**, and cosmetics containing plastic glitter, get transitional periods: rinse-off sellable until **16 Oct 2027**; leave-on until **16 Oct 2029**; make-up, lip and nail products until **16 Oct 2035**, and from **17 Oct 2031** these must be labelled as containing microplastics.
  - Which bucket loose cosmetic body/face glitter falls in (leave-on 2029 vs make-up 2035) depends on how the product is classified: unconfirmed; tell learners "at the latest 2029 or 2035 depending on product type".
  - Not covered: glitter that is biodegradable, water-soluble, natural (unmodified) or inorganic (glass, metal, mica). Glitter fixed into a solid (glue, paint) or stuck on an article is also out of scope.
- Plant/cellulose glitter (e.g. eucalyptus cellulose) is the common alternative. Caveat: some contain coatings; early research suggests cellulose/mica glitter is not harmless to aquatic plants. Teach "lower-impact", not "eco-safe".

## 3. United Kingdom (optional)

- GB cosmetics law is Schedule 34 of the Product Safety and Metrology etc. (Amendment etc.) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019, closely based on 1223/2009 (same annex structure). Enforced by OPSS / Trading Standards. Source: legislation.gov.uk Schedule 34 (search only) https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukdsi/2019/9780111176368/schedule/34
- The EU microplastics/glitter restriction does NOT apply in Great Britain; it does apply in Northern Ireland. Secondary source only (MHA / search results): unconfirmed on gov.uk. GB has a separate microbeads ban for rinse-off products (not researched).

## 4. Patch testing and allergies

Key facts:
- Test any new product on the inner arm or elbow bend before painting a face. FDA: a dab on the arm "for a couple of days"; FDA Halloween update: watch 24-48 h. AAD's stricter version for skincare: twice a day for 7-10 days on a quarter-sized spot.
- Signs: redness, itching, swelling, rash. If it happens: wash off, don't use again, cool compress; see a doctor/dermatologist if severe or it doesn't settle.
- Snazaroo suggests a quick 20-minute test on a 1x2 inch patch inside the elbow for sensitive skin (manufacturer guidance, shorter than FDA's).
- Common allergen groups to mention: fragrance, preservatives, nickel (metal findings), PPD (black henna/hair dye), latex (liquid latex, some lash glues).

## 5. Hygiene

Key facts:
- One sponge per person; don't double-dip used sponges into paint; clean brushes between people; use a spatula to take product out of pots; wash hands before and after.
- Don't share eye makeup.
- Never paint over broken skin, cuts, sores, active eczema/rashes, cold sores, or anyone with an eye infection (conjunctivitis); paint around, or don't paint.
- Keep paint out of eyes and mouth; remove before bed.
- If a reaction: wash off, stop, pharmacist/GP (UK) or doctor/dermatologist (US); eye pain or vision change = urgent eye care.

Sources:
- "Hygiene Tips for Face Painters" (Mehron blog, loaded). https://www.mehron.com/mehron-blog/hygiene-tips-for-face-painters
  Spatula to prevent cross-contamination; sanitize products, tools and surfaces; clean brushes after each client.
- "How To Use Cosmetics Safely Around Your Eyes" (American Academy of Ophthalmology, 20 Aug 2024, loaded). https://www.aao.org/eye-health/tips-prevention/eye-makeup
  Never share eye makeup; glitter flakes get into the tear film and irritate/scratch eyes, especially for contact lens wearers; keep makeup off the waterline; remove before sleep; replace eye makeup every 3 months.
- Snazaroo FAQ (above): avoid mouth and eyes, wash hands before and after.

## 6. Removal

Key facts:
- Water-activated face paint (Snazaroo, most kids' paints): warm water and soap; baby wipes for leftovers. Some bright colors can stain briefly.
- Water-based pro paints: micellar water on a cotton pad works (Mehron recommends its micellar water for highly pigmented water-based paints).
- Cream/grease paints: oil-based cleanser or oil first, then wash.
- Alcohol-activated paints (e.g. Skin Illustrator) are pro SFX products: they need a dedicated remover (Skin Illustrator lists Telesis removers). They are activated with 99% isopropyl or a special activator, which is flammable. Not a beginner product.
- Don't use rubbing alcohol or solvent removers near the eyes (Mehron Spirit Gum Remover is labelled "not for use near the eyes"). Around eyes: gentle, oil-based or eye-safe remover; AAO suggests petroleum jelly or baby shampoo for eye makeup.

Sources:

- "Mehron Blank Canvas Micellar Water" (loaded). https://www.mehron.com/mehron-blank-canvas-micellar-water/

## 7. Face gems / rhinestone adhesives

Key facts:
- Use only adhesives made for skin: cosmetic/eyelash glue, spirit gum, or prosthetic adhesive (e.g. Pros-Aide). Many self-adhesive face gems need no extra glue.
- Lash glue is the only one designed for the lash line; keep spirit gum and its remover away from eyes.
- Some lash glues contain latex; latex-free versions exist (e.g. DUO latex-free). Ask about latex allergy. Pros-Aide is sold as non-latex (retailer description; manufacturer page not loaded: unconfirmed).
- Spirit gum can irritate; patch test; flammable until dry.
- Never superglue, nail glue or craft glue on skin. If superglue gets on skin: don't pull skin apart; soak in warm water or use oil/petroleum jelly. Near/in eye: rinse with lukewarm water if lids aren't stuck; if stuck, warm compress, no alcohol/acetone, call poison control.

Sources:
- "Super Glue" (Ontario Poison Centre, loaded). https://www.ontariopoisoncentre.ca/household-hazards-items/super-glue/
- Mehron Spirit Gum Remover (section 6).
- Pros-Aide retailer description (Silly Farm, search only; product page returned 404).
- DUO latex-free lash adhesive (retailer listings, search only; manufacturer page not loaded).

## 8. Markers, craft paints and other non-cosmetics

Key facts:
- Only products sold as cosmetics/face paint are made with skin-approved colors. FDA: colors must be approved for the intended use; "a color that's OK on your tough fingernails or your hair may not be OK on your skin".
- "Non-toxic" (ACMI AP seal) on markers and craft paint means safe for art use as intended, not tested as a cosmetic for skin.
- Sharpie's own website warns against using its markers on skin; permanent marker ink contains solvents such as xylene/toluene (poison centre).
- Craft acrylics form a plastic film, may contain pigments not approved for cosmetics, and are hard to remove: not for skin. No primary regulatory source specifically on acrylic paint on skin found (unconfirmed beyond the FDA approval principle).

Sources:
- FDA Novelty Makeup and Color Additives fact sheet (section 1).
- "Viral Sharpie beauty hack sparks debate over safety risks" (Fox News, 13 Jul 2025, loaded). https://www.foxnews.com/health/sharpie-lip-liner-trend-goes-viral-doctors-warn-potential-dangers
  Quotes Sharpie's site and a board-certified dermatologist; cites Northern New England Poison Center on solvents. Sharpie's own page not fetched: unconfirmed directly.
- ACMI AP seal: https://acmiart.org/about-us (search only): certifies art materials as non-toxic "when used ... for their intended purpose".

## 9. Henna and "black henna"

- Real henna stains reddish-brown and is FDA-approved only as a hair dye. Anything sold as "black henna" (or that stains black within an hour) likely contains PPD, which can cause blistering, scarring and lifelong allergy to hair dyes. Do not use it, and do not offer it.
- Source: FDA henna fact sheet (section 1).

## 10. Children

- Snazaroo recommends face painting from age 3; for younger children only small designs on cheeks, hands or arms. https://www.snazaroo.com/pages/faq
- American Academy of Pediatrics: prefer non-toxic makeup and hats over masks; test makeup on a small patch of the child's skin first. "Halloween Fun & Safety Tips for Kids of All Ages" (HealthyChildren.org, updated 9/29/2026, loaded). https://www.healthychildren.org/english/safety-prevention/all-around/pages/halloween-safety-tips.aspx
- Kohl on children's eyes causes lead poisoning (FDA kohl page).
- No glitter near children's eyes; keep loose glitter and gems away from toddlers (gems are a choking hazard: common sense, not from a source).
- Get a parent/guardian's OK and ask about allergies and skin conditions before painting a child (industry practice; no primary source confirmed).

## Notes / discrepancies

- FDA lists fluorescent colors differently on two pages: the fact sheet names 7 (no D&C Yellow No. 7); Novelty Makeup names 8 (adds D&C Yellow No. 7). Safe teaching line: "No fluorescent/neon colors near the eyes."
- FDA lacks a current consumer page on glitter, spirit gum and lash adhesives. Do not claim specific FDA statements on these.
- US law change worth knowing (not verified in this pass): the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act 2022 (MoCRA) added facility registration and serious-adverse-event reporting for cosmetic companies. Unconfirmed details; not needed for a beginner course.
